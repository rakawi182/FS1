Low-Dimensional Radiative–Humid Coupling
at a Tropical Coastal Observatory

A multi-variable Fourier decomposition of the ECMWF IFS-HRES 2017–2026 record
MJS · Jolotundo Observatory · East Java · Φ = −7.5220° λ = 112.5661° h = 28 m · IFS-HRES 2017-01-01 → 2026-09-28 · N = 85,392 h

Abstract

We decompose 85,392 hourly meteorological records from the ECMWF IFS-HRES reanalysis for a tropical coastal site (MJS Observatory, East Java, Indonesia) into an analytical Fourier basis of annual, diurnal, and cross-frequency terms. Out of 13 primary variables (precipitation, cloud cover, radiation, temperature, humidity, soil moisture, wind, pressure) we find that the entire Fourier coefficient matrix — 1,534 numbers in total — collapses onto just two orthogonal modes that together explain 90 % of variance. PC1 (82 %) defines an unambiguous radiative–humid axis, separating fast-responding radiation-driven variables (ET₀, VPD, T₂, S_h) from slower, thermally buffered humid variables (T_d, U₂, θ₀₇, θ₇₂₈, N_c). The same structure is recovered (i) without supervision via hierarchical clustering, (ii) after seasonal detrending — proving it is a daily-scale mode, not a monsoon artefact, and (iii) in two independent 4.8-year epochs (11/11 sign agreement, magnitude ratio 1.07), demonstrating temporal stability. A 2-year TCWV sub-record (only available from Sept 2024) independently confirms its membership in the humid cluster (r = 0.81 with T_d, r = −0.47 with VPD). We conclude that the local tropical climate is controlled by a single, physically constrained coupling — not by an arbitrary superposition of independent cycles.

SECTION 1Data and Methodology

All analyses are based on a continuous 9.74-year hourly record from two nearby IFS-HRES grid points, IDW-interpolated (inverse-square, p = 2) to the MJS Observatory target coordinates.

1.1 Fourier basis

Each variable X(t) is decomposed as

X(t) = X̄₀ + Σk=1..18 Akcos(k·ωAt + φk) + Σm=1..11 Bmcos(m·ωDt + ψm) + Σn Cncos(Ωnt + χn)

where ωA = 2π/(365.242·24) rad·h⁻¹ (annual), ωD = 2π/24 rad·h⁻¹ (diurnal), and Ωn = k·ωA + m·ωD are the 30 cross-frequency terms that encode the seasonal modulation of the diurnal cycle. The m = 12 diurnal harmonic is deliberately excluded: it sits exactly at the hourly Nyquist frequency and is therefore rank-deficient.

1.2 Estimators

Three independent solvers were cross-validated: chunked normal equations (reference), QR decomposition (numerically stable), and Bayesian Ridge (posterior σ per coefficient). Because N/P ≈ 717, the three solvers agree to machine precision — the system is fully data-dominated, and the posterior collapses onto the OLS solution.

SECTION 2PC1 Verification: the Radiative–Humid Axis

If PC1 is physical, splitting days by solar radiation should cleanly separate the remaining variables into two families with opposite signs. We test this twice: on raw daily data, and after removing the monthly climatology.

2.1 Raw split (top/bottom 25 % of daily G)

Sorting 3,558 days by total shortwave irradiance G, the top quartile (⟨G⟩ = 280 W m⁻²) is significantly sunnier than the bottom quartile (⟨G⟩ = 169 W m⁻²). Every variable moves in the physically expected direction with a large effect size (Cohen's d between 1.75 and 4.45).

Figure 1. Cohen's d for the raw top/bottom-G quartile split. Positive bars indicate the variable is larger on sunny days; negative bars, on cloudy days. All 9 hypothesized signs (▲ radiative family, ▼ humid family) are confirmed.

2.2 Detrended split (seasonal cycle removed)

To prove the effect is not a monsoon artefact, we recompute the split using ΔG = G − monthly climatology. This removes the annual cycle while preserving day-to-day variability. Effect sizes drop by 10–50 %, but all 9 signs persist. The ratio of detrended/raw Cohen's d traces the physical response time of each variable — from 0.90 for fast radiative quantities to 0.44 for deep soil moisture.

Figure 2. Detrended (ΔG-based) effect sizes. The systematic ordering of the detrended/raw ratio (S_h 0.90 → ET₀ 0.85 → T₂ 0.75 → VPD/U₂ 0.65 → SM07 0.60 → SM728 0.44) is a fingerprint of the underlying physics: fast variables respond mostly to daily weather, slow variables mostly to season.

Key finding · Section 2

After seasonal detrending, the radiative–humid split survives with large effect sizes (|d| = 0.87–3.81). PC1 is therefore a genuine daily-scale mode, not a manifestation of the monsoon.

SECTION 3Temporal Stability across Two Epochs

The record is split into two halves of ~4.8 years each (2017-01→2021-09 and 2021-10→2026-09). If PC1 is a stable feature of the local climate, its sign structure must be preserved in both halves.

3.1 Secular shift (per-epoch daily means)

Comparing epoch means reveals a coherent wetting signal in the second half. Nine of twelve variables shift with |t| > 3: the atmosphere and soils become more humid (T_d ↑ 0.71 °C, U₂ ↑ 2.76 %, SM07 ↑ 9.8 %, SM728 ↑ 13.0 %, N_c ↑ 5.9 %, P ↑ 20 %), while VPD falls by 11 % and wind weakens by 5 %. Notably, T₂ and ET₀ show no significant change, suggesting a humid buffering mechanism that compensates the +3 % increase in solar irradiance.

Figure 3. Secular shift between epochs (Welch t-statistic). Positive t = value increased in the second epoch. Colored bars: humid family (blue), radiative family (red), neutral (gray). Only T₂ and ET₀ remain statistically unchanged.

3.2 Stability of Cohen's d per epoch

Per epoch, we detrend seasonally and compute the detrended Cohen's d. Then we compare the two values side-by-side.

Figure 4. Cohen's d per epoch (detrended). All 11 variables preserve their sign; the mean ratio |dlate|/|dearly| is 1.07, well inside the 0.70–1.40 stability band chosen for short epochs.

Key finding · Section 3

Despite a robust wetting trend between epochs, the strength and sign of the PC1 radiative–humid coupling remain unchanged. This separation of "mean shift" from "structure shift" is a hallmark of a physically constrained mode.

SECTION 4Multivariate Structure of the Fourier Coefficients

Stack the 118 Fourier coefficients of 13 variables into a 13 × 118 matrix and ask: what is its intrinsic dimensionality?

4.1 Effective dimensionality

Column-wise z-scoring is applied first (each coefficient position contributes equally). PCA then reveals an extremely concentrated spectrum: PC1 alone explains 82 % of variance, PC1+PC2 explain 90 %, and three components suffice for 95 %. Despite 1,534 numbers in the original matrix, the system is effectively 2–3 dimensional.

Figure 5. Cumulative variance explained by PCA components. The knee between PC2 and PC3 marks the transition from structural signal to residual noise.

4.3 PCA loadings — what is PC1?

PC1 (82 % of variance) loads positively on G, ET₀, S_h, T₂, e_d and negatively on U₂, T_d, N_c, θ₀₇, θ₇₂₈. This is the Fourier-domain image of the same radiative–humid axis identified in §2: fast radiative quantities move one way, slow humid quantities move the other.

Key finding · Section 4

The 13-variable system collapses to two meaningful modes. The first mode is a radiative–humid axis that appears identically in the PCA, in the hierarchical clustering, in the raw quartile split (§2.1), in the detrended split (§2.2), and in both temporal epochs (§3). Five independent tests, one physical answer.

SECTION 5Total Column Water Vapour — a Special Case

TCWV is only available in the IFS-HRES record from September 2024 onward, yielding 18,216 valid hours (~2.1 years). We therefore analyse it separately, restricting ourselves to quantities that are statistically well-posed over that window.

5.1 Seasonal envelope

Monthly means oscillate between 38.5 kg m⁻² (July–August) and 58.8 kg m⁻² (December), a seasonal contrast of 20.3 kg m⁻² — roughly 40 % of the annual mean. This is the largest relative seasonal amplitude of any variable in the study, and it directly reflects the Austral–Asian monsoon moisture transport.

Figure 6. Monthly mean TCWV over the 2-year sub-record (Sept 2024 – Sept 2026). Peak in December, trough in July–August — the signature of the monsoon cycle.

5.2 Diurnal cycle — very weak

A diurnal-only Fourier fit yields R² = 0.011 for the full m = 1..11 basis. Only the m = 1 harmonic is significant (A = 1.49 kg m⁻², 3 % of the mean, peak at 20:28 WIB); all higher harmonics are indistinguishable from noise. This confirms the physical expectation: column-integrated water vapour is insensitive to diurnal surface forcing — only the vertical distribution changes, not the total column.

5.3 Correlation with the other variables

Using the overlap window (18,209 common hourly samples), TCWV correlates strongly and positively with the humid cluster and moderately negatively with VPD.

Figure 7. Hourly Pearson correlation between TCWV and the other 12 variables. Bars are colored by the cluster assignment from §4.2: blue = humid, red = radiative, gray = independent. TCWV is unambiguously humid.

Key finding · Section 5

Even though TCWV was excluded from the multivariate analysis (only 21 % coverage), its hourly correlation structure independently confirms its membership in Cluster 1 — the humid family. It is strongly positively correlated with T_d (r = 0.81), θ₀₇ (0.76), θ₇₂₈ (0.65), N_c (0.59), U₂ (0.58), and anti-correlated with VPD (r = −0.47). No radiative variable correlates with it (|r| < 0.17).

Physical interpretation

The radiative–humid mode is the signature of surface-energy-driven convection in a tropical maritime climate. On clear days, high incoming shortwave radiation drives rapid surface warming and evaporative demand; VPD rises, humidity falls, and soil moisture is depleted. On cloudy days the cycle reverses: reduced radiation cools the surface, humidity accumulates, and soil moisture is replenished. The same coupling, viewed through the Fourier lens, appears as a single mode because the daily cycle dominates the variance of all fast variables and is modulated coherently by the annual cycle.

The observed 2017–2026 wetting trend (T_d ↑ 0.71 °C, U₂ ↑ 2.76 %, SM728 ↑ 13.0 %) does not distort this mode — it simply shifts its baseline. The magnitude ratio of 1.07 between epochs proves that the mode's intrinsic strength is conserved even as the mean state evolves. This is the signature of a physically constrained, rather than a statistically emergent, structure.

What this means for climate monitoring

If only 2–3 numbers are needed to describe the local climate's variability, then monitoring at MJS Observatory can be reduced, in principle, to tracking (i) the mean state and (ii) the amplitude and phase of the radiative–humid mode. This has practical implications for low-cost sensor networks and for downscaling climate projections to coastal tropical sites.
